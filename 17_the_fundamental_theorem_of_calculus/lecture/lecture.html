The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

Dr. Aamir Alaud Din

Objectives

After preparing this topic, you should be able to:

  1. Explain the first part of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and use it to differentiate functions defined by definite integrals with variable upper limits.
  2. Apply the second part of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to evaluate definite integrals using antiderivatives.
  3. Explain how differentiation and integration act as inverse processes and use this relationship to solve mathematical and engineering problems.

The Why Section

  • In mechanical engineering, sensors often record a changing quantity such as velocity, force, or temperature. Engineers may need to determine the accumulated effect of that quantity over a time interval and also understand how the accumulated quantity changes at a particular instant.

  • For example, the distance travelled by a moving mechanism can be obtained by accumulating its velocity over time. Conversely, if the position of the mechanism is known as a function of time, differentiation gives its instantaneous velocity.

  • Definite integrals provide a way to calculate accumulated quantities, while derivatives describe instantaneous rates of change. However, evaluating an integral directly from its definition can require a large number of calculations.

  • The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus establishes a connection between these two operations. Its first part explains how differentiating an accumulation function recovers the original continuous function. Its second part provides a practical method for evaluating definite integrals using an antiderivative.

  • This relationship is also useful in mechatronics and computer science. A controller may use sensor measurements of velocity to estimate a robot’s displacement, while a known position function can be differentiated to estimate its velocity. The same mathematical relationship appears in signal processing, simulation, and numerical modelling.

  • Therefore, studying this topic gives us a systematic way to move between rates of change and accumulated quantities and allows many definite integrals to be evaluated without constructing Riemann sums.

Objective 1: The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1

An Accumulation Function

  • Let f be a continuous function on the interval [a,b]. We define a new function by integrating f from a fixed lower limit a to a variable upper limit x.

g(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt \qquad a\leq x\leq b

  • The variable t is the variable of integration. It is a dummy variable, so it can be replaced by another symbol without changing the integral.

  • The value of g(x) represents the accumulated signed area under the graph of f from a to x. Areas above the horizontal axis contribute positively, while areas below the axis contribute negatively.

  • As x changes, the amount accumulated also changes. If f is positive, increasing the upper limit adds positive area; if f is negative, increasing the upper limit adds negative signed area.

Figure 1. The area under the graph of y=f(t) from the fixed lower limit a to the variable upper limit x.

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1

Theorem

If f is continuous on [a,b] and

g(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt

then g is continuous on [a,b], differentiable on (a,b), and

g'(x)=f(x)

\blacksquare

  • The theorem says that if we define a function by accumulating the values of a continuous function through integration, then differentiating that accumulation function gives us the original function.

  • In Leibniz notation, the result can be written as

\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt=f(x)

  • This result is particularly useful because the integral may define a function even when the integral cannot be expressed conveniently using elementary functions.

  • For example, if g(x)=\int_0^x \sqrt{1+t^2}\,dt, the Fundamental Theorem allows us to find g'(x) directly without first evaluating the integral.

Understanding the Result

  • Suppose g(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt. The difference g(x+h)-g(x) is the signed area accumulated between x and x+h.

g(x+h)-g(x)=\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\,dt

  • Dividing by h gives the average value of f over the short interval, with the orientation of the interval taken into account.

\frac{g(x+h)-g(x)}{h} =\frac{1}{h}\int_x^{x+h} f(t)\,dt

  • When h is close to zero and f is continuous, the values of f on this short interval are close to f(x). Thus the average value approaches f(x) as h approaches zero.

  • The left-hand side approaches the derivative g'(x). Therefore, g'(x)=f(x), which is the central conclusion of Part 1.

Example 1

If f is the function whose graph is shown in Figure 2 and g(x)=\int_0^x f(t)\,dt, find the values of g(0), g(1), g(2), g(3), g(4), and g(5). Then sketch a rough graph of g.

Figure 2. The graph of y=f(t) used to determine accumulated signed areas.

Solution

  • Since the upper and lower limits are equal, the integral is zero.

g(0)=\int_0^0 f(t)\,dt=0

  • From Figure 3, the area from 0 to 1 is a triangle with base 1 and height 2. Therefore,

g(1)=\int_0^1 f(t)\,dt=\frac12(1)(2)=1

  • From 1 to 2, the graph forms a rectangle with width 1 and height 2. Adding this area to the previous accumulated area gives

g(2)=g(1)+\int_1^2 f(t)\,dt=1+(1)(2)=3

  • From 2 to 3, we add the area under the curved portion of the graph. From the figure, this area is approximately 1.3.

g(3)=g(2)+\int_2^3 f(t)\,dt\approx3+1.3=4.3

  • For t>3, the graph lies below the horizontal axis. The signed area must therefore be subtracted. The area from 3 to 4 is approximately 1.3.

g(4)=g(3)+\int_3^4 f(t)\,dt\approx4.3-1.3=3.0

  • The area below the axis from 4 to 5 is approximately 1.3, so

g(5)=g(4)+\int_4^5 f(t)\,dt\approx3.0-1.3=1.7

  • The accumulated function increases while f is positive and decreases while f is negative. Its maximum occurs near the point where f changes from positive to negative.

Example 2

Find the derivative of the function

g(x)=\int_0^x \sqrt{1+t^2}\,dt

Solution

  • The integrand f(t)=\sqrt{1+t^2} is continuous for all real t. By Part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, we differentiate the accumulation function by evaluating the integrand at the upper limit.

g'(x)=\sqrt{1+x^2}

\blacksquare

Example 3

The Fresnel function is defined by

S(x)=\int_0^x \sin\left(\frac{\pi t^2}{2}\right)\,dt

Find S'(x).

Solution

  • The integrand is continuous, so Part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem applies directly.

S'(x)=\sin\left(\frac{\pi x^2}{2}\right)

  • The integral defines a function whose derivative can be found even though the integral does not generally have an elementary antiderivative.

When the Upper Limit Is a Function

  • Sometimes the upper limit is not simply x but a differentiable function of x. In that case, the Chain Rule must be used along with Part 1.

  • If

G(x)=\int_a^{u(x)} f(t)\,dt

then

G'(x)=f(u(x))u'(x)

Example 4

Find

\frac{d}{dx}\int_1^{x^4}\sec t\,dt

Solution

  • Let u=x^4. By the Chain Rule and Part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem,

\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dx}\int_1^{x^4}\sec t\,dt &=\sec(x^4)\frac{d}{dx}(x^4)\\ &=4x^3\sec(x^4) \end{aligned}

\blacksquare

G'(x)=x^3+1

Using the Chain Rule,

Objective 2: The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 2

Evaluating Definite Integrals with Antiderivatives

  • The first part of the Fundamental Theorem connects an accumulation function with its derivative. The second part gives a practical method for evaluating definite integrals.

  • Suppose f is continuous on [a,b] and F is any antiderivative of f, meaning that F'(x)=f(x). Then the definite integral is the difference between the values of F at the endpoints.

Theorem

If f is continuous on [a,b] and F'(x)=f(x) on the interval, then

\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)

\blacksquare

  • The expression F(b)-F(a) is sometimes written as [F(x)]_a^b. To evaluate a definite integral, we find an antiderivative, substitute the upper endpoint, subtract the value at the lower endpoint, and simplify.

  • This method avoids calculating the limit of Riemann sums for each integral. Any antiderivative may be used because the constant of integration cancels when the endpoint values are subtracted.

Example 5

Evaluate the integral

\int_1^3 e^x\,dx

Solution

  • An antiderivative of e^x is F(x)=e^x. Applying Part 2 gives

\begin{aligned} \int_1^3 e^x\,dx &=F(3)-F(1)\\ &=e^3-e \end{aligned}

\blacksquare

Interpreting the Definite Integral

  • The definite integral represents signed accumulation over an interval. If the graph is above the horizontal axis, the corresponding contribution is positive; if it is below the axis, the contribution is negative.

  • In a motion problem, if v(t) is velocity and s'(t)=v(t), then

\int_a^b v(t)\,dt=s(b)-s(a)

  • Thus the integral of velocity gives displacement, not necessarily total distance travelled. When velocity changes sign, the displacement accounts for direction, whereas total distance requires accumulating the absolute value of velocity.

Quick Check

Question 1

Evaluate \int_0^2 3x^2\,dx using an antiderivative.

Answer

  • An antiderivative of 3x^2 is F(x)=x^3. Therefore,

\int_0^2 3x^2\,dx=2^3-0^3=8

Question 2

If F'(x)=f(x), is \int_a^b f(x)\,dx equal to F(a)-F(b) or F(b)-F(a)?

Answer

  • The upper endpoint value is subtracted by the lower endpoint value, so the correct expression is

\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)

\blacksquare

Objective 3: Differentiation and Integration as Inverse Processes

The Relationship Between the Two Operations

  • Differentiation measures the instantaneous rate of change of a function, while integration accumulates the values of a function over an interval. The Fundamental Theorem shows that these processes are closely related.

  • If f is continuous and we define g(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt, Part 1 states that g'(x)=f(x). In this sense, differentiation reverses the accumulation represented by the integral.

  • Part 2 states that a definite integral can be evaluated using an antiderivative. If F'=f, then integrating f from a to b gives the net change F(b)-F(a).

  • The two statements together explain why differentiation and integration are often described as inverse processes. They are not identical operations in every context, but the theorem establishes the precise relationship under its continuity and differentiability conditions.

Differentiating an Integral and Integrating a Derivative

  • If f is continuous, then

\frac{d}{dx}\left(\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\right)=f(x)

  • If F is differentiable and F'=f, then

\int_a^b F'(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)

  • The first formula recovers the integrand from its accumulation function. The second formula recovers the net change in a function by integrating its derivative.

  • For example, if the position of a robot is s(t)=t^3+2t, then its velocity is v(t)=3t^2+2. Integrating this velocity from t=a to t=b gives the difference between the final and initial positions.

\int_a^b (3t^2+2)\,dt=(b^3+2b)-(a^3+2a)

  • This identity is useful in engineering because rates of change are often measured by sensors, while accumulated changes are needed to estimate positions, total energy, or other quantities.

Why the Constant of Integration Does Not Matter

  • An indefinite integral gives a family of antiderivatives that differ by a constant. If F'(x)=f(x), then F(x)+C is also an antiderivative.

  • For a definite integral, the constant cancels when we subtract the endpoint values.

[F(b)+C]-[F(a)+C]=F(b)-F(a)

  • Therefore, any antiderivative of the integrand can be used in Part

\int_1^3 F'(x)\,dx=F(3)-F(1)=13-5=8

Scenario Problem

Problem

A small autonomous mobile robot moves along a straight test track. Its measured velocity is modelled by

v(t)=3t^2+2

where v is measured in metres per second and t is measured in seconds. The robot’s initial position is s(0)=1 metre. Use the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus to determine the robot’s displacement between t=0 and t=2 seconds and its position at t=2 seconds.

Solution

  • The displacement is the definite integral of velocity over the time interval.

\begin{aligned} \Delta s &=\int_0^2 (3t^2+2)\,dt\\ &=[t^3+2t]_0^2\\ &=(8+4)-0\\ &=12\text{ m} \end{aligned}

  • The final position equals the initial position plus the displacement.

s(2)=s(0)+\Delta s=1+12=13\text{ m}

  • Therefore, the robot travels a displacement of 12 metres and reaches a position of 13 metres from the chosen origin at t=2 seconds.

\blacksquare

Summary

  • The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus connects differential calculus with integral calculus.
  • Part 1 states that if f is continuous and g(x)=\int_a^x f(t)\,dt, then g'(x)=f(x).
  • The accumulation function represents signed area from a fixed lower endpoint to a variable upper endpoint.
  • When the upper limit is a differentiable function, the Chain Rule must be used with Part 1.
  • Part 2 states that a definite integral can be evaluated by subtracting the antiderivative’s value at the lower endpoint from its value at the upper endpoint.
  • Areas below the horizontal axis contribute negatively to a definite integral.
  • The integral of velocity gives displacement, while total distance may require integrating the absolute value of velocity.
  • Differentiation recovers the integrand of an accumulation function, and integration of a derivative gives the net change in the original function.
  • The constant of integration cancels when endpoint values are subtracted in a definite integral.
